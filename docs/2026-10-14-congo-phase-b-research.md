# Congo Phase B species: published parameters and assumptions

This note gives source values for four species proposed for the western Congo Basin forest (Gabon and Republic of Congo): the forest elephant, a semi-terrestrial monkey, the crowned eagle and a forest crocodile. It follows the format of `2026-10-13-congo-species-sources.md`.

**Assumptions.** Every figure marked **A** is a game assumption, not a measurement. The reasoning is given next to it. Bracketed numbers point to the reference list at the end.

**Bounty eligibility (IUCN).** Of the four recommended species, only the agile mangabey (Least Concern) can be a bounty target. The forest elephant is Critically Endangered and the dwarf crocodile is Vulnerable. The crowned eagle is Near Threatened, which is not in the excluded set (CR/EN/VU). Whether NT species can be targets is a design call.

**Gaps in the sources.**
- Forest data on running, flight and swim speeds do not exist for any of the four species. All speeds are **A**.
- Daily food intake has not been measured in the wild for the elephant, the mangabey or the crocodile.
- Crowned eagle diet data come from Taï (Côte d'Ivoire) and Kibale/Ngogo (Uganda). No nest study from Gabon or Congo was found.
- The agile mangabey is poorly studied. Ranging and diet values come from Animal Diversity Web and zoo literature, plus Lopé data for the related grey-cheeked mangabey.
- Some values were read from abstracts or from secondary summaries that cite a primary paper (marked "via"). These include White et al. 1993, Struhsaker & Leakey 1990, Mitani et al. 2001 and McGraw et al. 2006.

## 1. Forest elephant (*Loxodonta cyclotis*)

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder height | Dzanga: females average 2.0 m (range 1.5–2.4), males 2.3 m (1.6–2.8). No elephant in a Gabon sample was taller than 2.16 m (Morgan & Lee 2003). | [8], [11] |
| Body mass | Fully grown males in good condition average about 2.0 t and 2.2 m. The largest reach about 2.75 m and 3.5 t (Larramendi 2016). The species range given is 2.7–6 t, but the forest figures are weak. **A:** female 1.6 t, male 2.5 t. | [7], [8] |
| Diet | Lopé: at least 307 food items. Leaves and bark make up 70% of items recorded, and trees 73% of species eaten. Fruit of at least 72 species; fruit remains in 82% of 311 dung piles. **A:** by mass, about 40% fruit, 45% browse and bark, 15% herbs, grasses and bai soil. Fruit share rises in fruiting peaks. | [6] |
| Food intake | No forest measurement. **A:** 4–6% of body mass a day as wet food, so 80–120 kg for a 2 t animal (scaled from savanna elephants). | — |
| Social | Small family units of a mother and her offspring. Males leave from about 7 years old and live alone or in loose groups. Lopé groups held 3–8 animals. **A:** mean group 3 (range 1–8), with groups merging at bais. | [1], [6] |
| Home range | 28 GPS-collared elephants: 95% kernel 7.7–2,402 km² (mean 264). Minimum convex polygon 26–2,226 km² (mean 547). Range size grows with the area of roadless forest. | [4] |
| Daily movement | GPS fixes: 1.0–2.5 km/day in normal ranging; 24 km/day when crossing an unprotected road. Family groups travel about 7.8 km/day (Turkalo & Barnes 2013). **A:** 2–8 km/day. | [4], [11] |
| Density, per km² | Gabon nationwide 0.38 (95% CI 0.24–0.52), 95,110 elephants (DNA spatial capture–recapture). Central Africa lost about 62% of its elephants in 2002–2011. | [3], [10] |
| Reproduction | Gestation 22 months (~670 days); 1 calf. Median age at first birth 23 years (youngest 10). Median interval between calves 59 months (Dzanga, n = 212), or 68 months in the 2017 analysis. Births 5.3%/yr, deaths 3.5%/yr. | [1], [2] |
| Male maturity | Not reported for the forest species. **A:** sexually mature at ~15 years; breeds successfully from ~25 years. | — |
| Lifespan | Oldest female estimated at 75 years at death; oldest mother 59. **A:** maximum 65 years in the sim. | [1] |
| Activity | Active day and night. Bais are visited by day and at night. **A:** cathemeral, rests 4–6 h a day. | [9] |
| Speeds | **A:** walk 1.0–1.5 m/s; charge 5–7 m/s over short distances; swims readily across rivers at ~0.5 m/s. | — |
| Predators | Adults: humans only. **A:** a leopard can take an unattended calf under 1 year, but this is very rare; calf survival in year 1 is over 97% [1]. The eagle and crocodiles take none. | [1] |
| Water and habitat | Uses terra firme forest, swamps and bais. At Dzanga, elephants were present in more than 99% of 1,800 observation hours; the clearing's water is rich in calcium and other cations. **A:** drinks daily and visits mineral bais every few days. | [9] |
| IUCN | **Critically Endangered** (Gobush et al. 2021). **Not a bounty target.** | [5] |

**Interactions with the existing species.** The elephant is the forest's main large-seed disperser and "engineer". **A:** forest buffalo, sitatunga and red river hogs use its trails and the clearings it keeps open at bais. It knocks down and drops fruit that blue, bay and Peters's duikers and red river hogs pick up off the floor. **A:** under fruiting trees, duikers and hogs gain food where an elephant has fed. Elephants compete with forest buffalo for herbs and browse at bais and in clearings. Mostly they displace the buffalo rather than fight. **A:** elephants trample the edges of swamps used by sitatunga and water chevrotains and open water channels, but cause no direct mortality. Leopards and golden cats avoid elephants. Duikers and chevrotains can respond to an elephant's alarm rumble as a cue (**A**).

## 2. Semi-terrestrial monkey: recommendation

| Candidate | Ground use | Gabon/Congo | IUCN | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agile mangabey** (*Cercocebus agilis*) | 12–22% of time on the ground; feeds and travels in the understorey [12] | Gabon, Republic of Congo, CAR, Cameroon, DRC; swamp and periodically flooded forest [12], [13] | **Least Concern** (Maisels et al. 2020) [13] | **Recommended** |
| Grey-cheeked mangabey (*Lophocebus albigena*) | Almost wholly arboreal; rarely on the ground | Lopé, Odzala | **Vulnerable** (2020) [16] | Not a ground forager; not a bounty target |
| Mandrill (*Mandrillus sphinx*) | Mostly terrestrial | Lopé, southern Gabon, Congo | **Vulnerable** (2019) [18] | Hordes of 620 (up to 845) [17] using 182 km² over 6 years [19] are too large to simulate; not a bounty target |

**Recommendation: agile mangabey.** It is the only candidate that truly forages on both the ground and in the canopy. It is Least Concern, so it can be a bounty target. Its swamp and flooded-forest habitat overlaps the water chevrotain and sitatunga habitats the sim already has. It is also the genus most hunted by crowned eagles at Taï, where the ground-foraging sooty mangabey (*Cercocebus atys*) dominated the larger primate prey [22]. Its weakness is thin field data. Gaps below are filled from the grey-cheeked mangabey at Lopé and marked as such.

### Agile mangabey (*Cercocebus agilis*)

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Body mass | Males 7–13 kg; females 5–7 kg (Kingdon 1997). Females are ~60% of male mass. **A:** male 10 kg, female 6 kg. | [12], [14] |
| Length | Head and body 44–65 cm. **A:** shoulder height ~0.30 m when on all fours. | [12] |
| Diet | Fruit-dominated. At least 42 fruit species; also seeds, shoots, grasses, mushrooms, invertebrates, bird eggs and small vertebrates. 26–30% of feeding time is spent foraging for insects. Lopé grey-cheeked mangabeys spent 36% of feeding time on seeds. **A:** 60% fruit and seeds, 25% invertebrates, 10% leaves and shoots, 5% fungi and other. | [12], [13], [15] |
| Food intake | **A:** ~0.35 kg wet food a day for a 7 kg animal (~5% of body mass). | — |
| Social | Groups of 7–22, usually one adult male. Groups often split into subgroups and sometimes merge into "supergroups" (Mondika). **A:** mean group 15. | [12], [14], [36] |
| Home range | Just under 2 km² (1.98 km²). Lopé grey-cheeked groups used 1.56–2.25 km². | [12], [15] |
| Density, per km² | No published Gabon or Congo estimate found. **A:** 0.3–0.5 groups/km², or 4–8 animals/km², in swamp forest; lower on terra firme. | — |
| Reproduction | Gestation 165–175 days; 1 infant; mature at 4–5 years. The interbirth interval is unknown in the wild. **A:** 18–24 months. | [12] |
| Lifespan | Up to 20 years in the wild. **A:** maximum 25. | [12] |
| Activity | Diurnal. | [12] |
| Speeds | **A:** walk 0.8 m/s; run on the ground 5–6 m/s; flees up into trees when a cat is close, and down into undergrowth or dense mid-canopy when an eagle is close. | — |
| Predators | Leopards, crowned eagles and pythons (ADW). The leopard is documented as a major predator of primates at Taï and Lopé (diurnal primates were 18% of leopard prey biomass at Lopé). Eagles at Taï took mostly adult males of the large ground-foraging mangabey. | [12], [20], [21], [22] |
| Water and habitat | Mainly periodically flooded primary forest near fresh water; some groups in non-flooded mixed forest. **A:** drinks from streams; most of its water comes from fruit. | [12] |
| IUCN | **Least Concern** (Maisels et al. 2020, e.T136615A167735266). **Can be a bounty target.** | [13] |

**Interactions with the existing species.** The agile mangabey is mid-sized leopard prey, larger than a blue duiker and smaller than a bay duiker. Its ground foraging puts it in the leopard's ambush zone; it escapes upward. **A:** golden cats take juvenile mangabeys only. A golden cat's mean prey is ~1.4 kg and rodents are half its prey items in the Ituri [35]. No source checked here records golden cats taking mangabeys. Mangabey troops crack hard seeds and drop fruit, and duikers follow monkey troops to collect fallen fruit. **A:** blue and Peters's duikers gain feeding when a troop is overhead. Its alarm calls warn of leopards and eagles. **A:** duikers and chevrotains within ~50 m respond to mangabey alarms. It shares swamp forest with sitatunga and water chevrotains without competing; it competes with red river hogs for fallen fruit and invertebrates in leaf litter.

## 3. Crowned eagle (*Stephanoaetus coronatus*)

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Body mass | Female 3.2–4.7 kg; male 2.55–4.12 kg. Females are 10–15% larger. **A:** female 4.0 kg, male 3.2 kg. | [25] |
| Size | Length 80–99 cm; wingspan 1.51–1.81 m (largest female 1.9 m). | [25] |
| Diet, rainforest | Kibale: monkeys were 83.7% of prey (one pair, 3.25 yr). Ngogo: 81% monkeys, 66% of those red-tailed monkeys. Ituri: primates 88% of remains. Taï: of ~1,200 bones from 16 nests, 669 (56%) were primate. Taï eagles also showed a preference for small duikers. Duikers recorded as prey include the blue, red and common duikers. | [22], [23], [24], [25] |
| Prey mass | Typical prey is 1–5 kg. Mean prey at Taï was 5.67 kg, the highest of any living accipitrid. A third of Taï monkey prey weighed 6–11 kg, mostly mangabeys. Prey of up to ~20 kg is taken; young bushbuck of ~30 kg have been recorded. | [22], [25] |
| Prey selection | Kibale: took the adult males of four monkey species, but infants and juveniles of red colobus. Monkeys that associate with other species were taken less often. | [23] |
| Kill rate | After hatching, the male makes about one kill every 1.5 days. **A:** outside breeding, one prey of 3–6 kg every 3–4 days per bird, against a need of ~0.25 kg/day (~6% of body mass, scaled from golden eagle intake). | [25] |
| Hunting | Still-hunts from a perch, waits a long time, then drops onto prey. Most kills are made on the forest floor. It can lift prey almost vertically to a branch. | [25] |
| Social and territory | Pairs are territorial. Deep-forest hunting ranges are 6.5–16 km² per adult. Nests are on tall emergent trees: at Taï, nest trees averaged ~52 m, with nests at ~36 m. | [25], [27] |
| Density | **A:** one pair per 10–15 km² of continuous forest (from the hunting-range size). | — |
| Reproduction | Clutch of 1–2 eggs; only one chick is raised. Incubation ~49 days; fledging 90–115 days (mean 110.6). The young is fed for 270–350 days after hatching and depends on the adults for up to ~11 months. Breeds once every 2 years (~500-day cycle). Mature at ~5 years. | [25] |
| Lifespan | 14–16 years on average; some live more than 30 (Brown & Amadon 1986). | [25] |
| Activity | Diurnal. | [25] |
| Speeds | **A:** perch-drop stoop 15–20 m/s over 10–30 m; flapping flight 12–15 m/s; manoeuvres beneath the canopy. | — |
| Predators | No regular predator of adults is recorded. **A:** none in the sim; leopards or golden cats may take a grounded fledgling. | — |
| Habitat | Tall forest with emergent trees for nests; also gallery forest and forest–savanna mosaic. Needs no open water. | [25], [27] |
| IUCN | **Near Threatened** (since 2012; 2018 global listing) with 5,000–50,000 mature individuals, decreasing. Not in the CR/EN/VU exclusion. | [26] |

**Interactions with the existing species.** The crowned eagle is the only aerial predator in the sim and adds a predator the duikers cannot escape by running. **A:** blue duikers (3.5–9 kg) and newborn duikers of all species are primary eagle prey, and so are mangabeys. Peters's and bay duiker calves and water chevrotain calves are also within reach. Adult bay and Peters's duikers (15–25 kg) and adult chevrotains (~10–15 kg) are near or above its practical limit and are rarely taken (**A**). It takes no sitatunga, red river hog or buffalo beyond the youngest piglets (**A**). It competes with leopards and golden cats for primates and small duikers but does not interact with them directly. Blue duikers freeze or dash into cover when an eagle calls or a shadow passes (**A**).

## 4. Forest crocodile: recommendation

| Candidate | Habitat | Prey size | IUCN | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dwarf crocodile** (*Osteolaemus*; *O. tetraspis* in the Ogooué basin of Gabon, *O. osborni* in the Congo basin) | Forest streams, swamps and flooded forest; very terrestrial; hunts on forest trails at night [28], [29] | Invertebrates, fish, frogs and small vertebrates [28], [29] | *O. tetraspis* **Vulnerable** (1996 assessment, out of date); *O. osborni* not assessed separately [31], [32] | **Recommended** |
| Central African slender-snouted crocodile (*Mecistops leptorhynchus*) | Large rivers and lakes | Mostly fish; mammals occasionally | Not yet listed; regional draft says **Endangered**. The combined species was CR. [33], [34] | Large-river fish-eater; seldom meets the sim's forest animals |

**Recommendation: dwarf crocodile.** It lives in the forest streams, swamps and flooded forest the sim already models, and it moves overland on forest trails at night. It is common and widespread in Gabon: it was present in 6 of 9 surveyed national parks (Eaton 2010, via [28]). The slender-snouted crocodile lives in open, large rivers and mostly eats fish. Neither crocodile can be a bounty target. The dwarf crocodile is not a large predator: it should be an ambient predator of small prey, not a threat to duikers.

### Dwarf crocodile (*Osteolaemus tetraspis* / *O. osborni*)

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Body mass | Males 18–45 kg; females 13.6–31.8 kg. **A:** male 25 kg, female 18 kg (wild Gabon animals are small). | [28] |
| Length | Males 1.32–1.80 m total length; females 1.02–1.80 m. Largest wild animal in a Gabon sample of 617 measured 1.57 m. Maximum about 2 m. Mature at ~1 m. | [28] |
| Diet | Opportunistic. Invertebrates are the main food in most studies: millipedes, beetle larvae, crabs, shrimp, spiders, insects and snails. Also fish, frogs, reptiles, small mammals and birds. Congo swamp forest: varied invertebrates and small vertebrates in the dry season (Riley & Huchzermeyer 2000). **A:** 60% invertebrates (crabs, millipedes, insects), 20% fish, 15% frogs and reptiles, 5% small mammals and birds. | [28], [29] |
| Prey mass | **A:** 0.005–1 kg; the largest is rodent-sized (~2 kg). No duiker or chevrotain predation recorded. | — |
| Food intake / kill rate | **A:** ~1% of body mass a day on average (crocodilians have a low metabolic rate), so ~0.2 kg a day in many small items. | — |
| Social | Mostly solitary; sometimes seen in pairs or family groups. | [28] |
| Home range | Not studied in the wild. **A:** 0.5–2 km of stream plus the nearby forest floor. | [28] |
| Density | Gabon (unhunted): about 4.5 × Cameroon's 1.02 animals per km of stream, so ~4.6 per km (derived). Cameroon *O. osborni*: 0.61/km. Most animals in counts are juveniles. **A:** 3–5 per km of forest stream. | [30] |
| Reproduction | Mound nest of leaves and rotting vegetation. Clutch mean 10–14 eggs (range 6–17); eggs 57–71 g. Incubation ~100 days (68–126 in captivity). Hatchlings 44–51 g, 18–28 cm. Mature at ~5 years, or 6.8–15.7 years estimated in Gabon. **A:** one clutch a year at most, in the wet season. | [28] |
| Lifespan | Over 40 years in captivity is common. **A:** wild maximum 40. | [28] |
| Activity | Strictly nocturnal. Rests by day in pools, burrows or water. | [28], [32] |
| Speeds | One of the most terrestrial crocodiles; walks with its belly raised off the ground. **A:** walk 0.3–0.5 m/s; land lunge 3 m/s over 1–2 m; swims 0.5–1 m/s. | [28] |
| Predators | Eggs and juveniles are eaten by Nile monitors, pythons, soft-shelled turtles, otters, water mongooses, civets, genets, **leopards and golden cats**, herons and raptors. **A:** the crowned eagle takes young crocodiles rarely. Leopards can kill adults (no data). | [28] |
| Water and habitat | Slow, shaded streams 1–10 m wide and under 0.5 m deep; swamp and periodically flooded forest. Needs dense rotting vegetation to build nests. Some Gabon populations live in caves. | [28], [30] |
| IUCN | *O. tetraspis* **Vulnerable** (1996). **Not a bounty target.** | [31], [32] |

**Interactions with the existing species.** The dwarf crocodile mainly eats invertebrates and does not hunt the sim's ungulates. **A:** at most it takes a newborn chevrotain or duiker calf at the water's edge (very rare). It shares streams with the water chevrotain, which escapes predators by diving. **A:** the chevrotain's dive escape is unaffected by the crocodile. Leopards and golden cats eat its eggs and young. Sitatunga, red river hogs, buffalo and elephants cross its streams without interacting with it. **A:** a crocodile on a forest trail at night may startle a duiker, but it poses no risk.
